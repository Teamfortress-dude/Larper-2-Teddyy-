# Larper-2

The Larper-2 is a J-Class rocket motor producing around 1068 Ns of impulse and 710 Newtons of thrust. It is the second rocket motor in my Larper series and is better in every way compared to the Larper-1. The nickname Teddyy was given by a friend of mine. What differentiates it from other amateur KNSB rocket motors is the experimental thermal insulators that use a carbon fiber-reinforced 3D printing filament known as PET-CF17. This allows me to make very precise insulator sizes without making a mess by using epoxy and kraft paper.

This rocket motor is just one of many passion projects I have in mind to build a portfolio for my dream career of being a propulsion engineer. At the same time, I want to introduce my home country of Indonesia to amateur rocketry. Currently, it is an absolute pain to even get a static fire of a rocket motor because here, amateur rocketry is regulated similarly to weapons, which it obviously is not. I hope this project will bring enough awareness so aspiring rocketeers can actually live out their dreams.

If you have any questions regarding the specific math equations used to design this rocket motor, you can check the JOURNAL.MD file or email me at justinbmcclure@gmail.com. # Casing processing

1. Take your 45 cm 6061-T6 pipe and draw 2 lines that are spaced 34.9 cm apart. Ensure you cut off the ends, as they may be dented during shipping or storage.

2. Use a hacksaw and cut where you marked.

3. Use sandpaper (400 grit to 1500) to level and smooth out the freshly cut end; ensure you go from rough to fine. You can use the cut ends to practice manually trying to level with sandpaper.

4. Using a 3D-printed jig, drill 8 radial holes 1.5 cm inward from the ends of the pipe. Do this for both ends.

5. Carefully sand the edges of the 16 holes with high grit (1000 to 1500) to clean up the holes. 

# Assembly of motor

**This assembly assumes you have the KNSB grains already cast and the annealed PET-CF17 liner. Further information regarding these two components will be added once I begin assembling this project myself, as there are a lot of unknowns that I can only confirm with experimentation.**

1. First, we must prepare the nozzle. This part consists of 2 pieces: a nozzle insert and a nozzle closure. A thin but even coating of RTV silicone is applied to both the interior of the nozzle closure and the exterior of the nozzle insert. Follow the picture to see where the silicone must be applied. Once applied, you should stand it upright and place heavy books or weights on top of the insert to ensure everything seals as the silicone cures and that there are no voids.

<img width="740" height="837" alt="Nozzle Assembly" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/2a20522c-1273-4f6b-b2b9-878492a0f6a0" />

2. Second, once the silicone has cured, we need to add the seals. To ensure no tearing or shearing, apply a generous amount of silicone lubricant in the O-ring grooves as well as on the cylindrical face of the nozzle closure. Now, take 2 G56 FKM O-rings and fit each of them into their O-ring grooves.

<img width="782" height="619" alt="O-rings" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/a9487e85-b903-4fd0-8dee-e627d22b3812" />

3. Third, we need to bolt the nozzle closure into place. Slide the assembled nozzle closure into one end of the casing and line up the M6 screw holes with the drilled holes on the casing. Once lined up, place a 1 mm M6 copper washer on an M6x14 screw. This washer is here to ensure the steel doesn't bite into the casing. Once ready, insert the screw into the hole. Now do this for all 8 screws.

<img width="1011" height="802" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/26211a7d-e24d-4613-93c9-3c993b9190aa" />

4. Fourth, insert propellant. To start, begin by inserting all 3 KNSB grains into the PET-CF17 liner. Be sure you do **NOT** glue the grains to the liner, as they need to move during the burn. Ensure there is a somewhat tight friction fit. A good rule of thumb is to see if you can push the grain in with only your hands and that, when you tap the liner and propellant assembly vertically on a surface, the grain does not move. These two parameters check whether your parts are too big or too small. Please remake your parts if the parts are too loose or too tight to ensure a safe burn. Once all grains have been inserted, take the whole assembly and lather a generous amount of silicone lubricant on the exterior. Use your hands to ensure the whole surface is coated. Now slide this assembly into the casing and be sure to push the grains and casing independently so the liner can sleeve around the nozzle closure and so the grains are seated on the nozzle insert.

<img width="1387" height="408" alt="Screenshot 2026-11-14 195452" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/74e57a4b-1ac8-4727-9de1-ce2ecf9fe8a9" />

5. Fifth, we need to prep the bulkhead. Just like the nozzle, apply a generous amount of silicone lubricant to the cylindrical surface and O-ring grooves of the bulkhead. Now, take 2 G56 FKM O-rings and fit each of them into their O-ring grooves. 

<img width="316" height="273" alt="Screenshot 2026-11-14 201915" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/22dcb396-59b8-4afc-9fd9-7a20462ada1d" />

6. Finally, slide the bulkhead into the open end of the casing. Ensure you line up the M6 screw holes with the casing holes. If you followed step 4 properly, the holes should already be at the same level when placed into the casing. Just like the nozzle, prepare your M6x14 screws and M6 1mm copper washers to screw the bulkhead in. Place a piece of tape on the nozzle hole to keep moisture out. Congrats, you have assembled the Larper-2.

# Possible Issue

Since this uses a new and experimental liner with relatively unknown performance, the casing may get hotter than expected, meaning you might need to replace the casing. This is exactly what this rocket motor is designed to test.
